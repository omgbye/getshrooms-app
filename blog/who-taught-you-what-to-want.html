Who taught you what to want?

I build software for a living — web design, graphics, photography, years of making things for screens. So when I went looking for an app to help me figure out my own life, I did it the way you'd expect: I downloaded all of them.

Every habit tracker. Every streak machine. Every cheerful little system that promised to optimize me if I would just check the box, close the ring, keep the chain alive. A million apps, a dime a dozen, all of them technically competent and all of them, in my hands, completely dead. I didn't enjoy a single one. Worse — I didn't feel like I needed any of them. They were slot machines for self-improvement: pull the lever, get the confetti, feel briefly like a person who has their life together, learn absolutely nothing about yourself. The experience, honestly, was terrible. And I say that as somebody who builds experiences for a living.

So I started taking the problem apart the way an engineer takes anything apart. Forget the interface. Forget the streaks. What is the actual failure? And somewhere in that teardown a different question showed up — not a feature request, not a user story. A question I couldn't shake:

Who taught you what to want?

Nobody is born wanting a corner office, a specific car, a job title that lands well at reunions. I wasn't. Those wants were installed — at the family table, where certain futures got praised and others got quietly pitied. At school, where success shrank down to a few respectable shapes. Then the feed took over the night shift: thousands of images a day of other people's highlight reels, each one whispering that this, exactly this, is what a life is supposed to look like. By the time you're old enough to choose, most of the choosing has been done for you. You're not running your ambitions. You're running factory settings and calling it a personality.

The first component was a question, not a tracker

Here's the thing nobody in my industry wants to admit: the tracker was never the product. A tracker assumes your goals are fine and only your discipline is broken. But what if the goals themselves are the bug — inherited code, running unexamined since childhood? No amount of streaks fixes that. You just become extremely consistent at chasing somebody else's life.

So the first component I built wasn't a tracker. It was a question — a structured trip through your interests, your values, your actual history, designed to do one unfashionable thing: separate the wants you inherited from the wants that are actually yours. There's a test I keep coming back to, and it's brutal in its simplicity: would you still want this life if nobody ever knew you did it? If the wanting collapses when the audience leaves, you never wanted the thing. You wanted the witnessing.

Turning a mushroom into software

People ask about the name, so let's get it out of the way. A mushroom is just the part you can see. The real organism is the mycelium — a vast hidden network underground, connecting trees to soil, moving nutrients and information across a whole forest, in the dark, without asking for credit. That's not a bad description of a human life, when you think about it. The visible career, the title, the bio — that's the fruit. Everything that actually feeds it is underground: the skills nobody saw you build, the curiosities you followed for no reason, the people who quietly rerouted your life. I wanted to build software shaped like that. Whimsical name. Serious instrument.

So SHROOMS is built as components that mirror the organism. A trip — the questions. A reveal — the moment the pattern underground becomes visible, the map of where you actually are. A guide — an AI coach that asks better questions than your feed does. And small real-world experiments instead of streaks, because a life isn't proven by checking a box; it's proven by going outside and touching the thing. The daily step matters, but it's the last component, not the first. First you find out what's yours. Then you track it.

Not inventing the wheel

I want to be honest about what this is, because the app store is full of people who aren't. I did not invent the wheel. Questions are old. Journals are old. Mentors are old. People have been stepping outside their own programming to look at their lives clearly for thousands of years, using whatever techniques their century had on hand. What I did — all any engineer ever really does — is take pieces that already existed and assemble them toward a moment nobody in my industry seems to be designing for: the epiphany. The eureka. That half-second when something true about your own life clicks into place and the room looks different.

You can't manufacture that moment. Anyone who promises to is lying to you. But you can build the room it walks into. You can design the questions, the data, the timing — dare I say it, a synchronization of spiritual wisdom with technology — so that when a person is ready to see their own life clearly, the instrument is in their hand. That's the goal. Not another tracker. A step forward, for somebody, the way somebody's offhand question once was for me.

It's in beta. It's unfinished, like everyone who'll ever use it. But the first component works, and it's free, and it starts with the only question that ever mattered: who taught you what to want — and what do you want, now that you know?
