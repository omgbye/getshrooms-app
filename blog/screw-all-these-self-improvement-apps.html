Screw All These Self-Improvement Apps That Do Nothing for You

I downloaded the apps. I tried the systems. Then I decided to build something I actually wanted to use.

I have a particular frustration with the self-improvement industry, especially its software.

There are thousands of applications promising to improve your productivity, discover your purpose, develop better habits, or somehow transform your life. Download one, answer a few questions, admire the interface, and inevitably discover that the most interesting features require another subscription.

And after all that, what have you actually learned about yourself?

In my experience, remarkably little.

My background is in web development, graphic design, and photography. I've spent years creating digital experiences, so I appreciate the engineering behind these products. But I also recognize when an attractive interface is compensating for a lack of substance.

I wasn't looking for another application to remind me to drink water or congratulate me for opening it seven consecutive days. I wanted something that could challenge my assumptions, introduce possibilities I hadn't considered, and perhaps help me understand why I wanted certain things in the first place.

So I started building SHROOMS.

Turning a mushroom into software

The name was deliberately unconventional. But what began as a whimsical concept evolved into something more interesting from an engineering perspective.

A mushroom is the visible expression of a much larger biological system. Beneath it exists mycelium, a network of interconnected filaments. I became fascinated with translating that structure into software.

What if someone's interests, abilities, ambitions, experiences, and contradictions could be explored as interconnected information rather than reduced to a generic personality label?

That idea became the foundation of SHROOMS: structured questions, career domains, visual relationships, and recommendations designed to encourage exploration rather than dictate someone's future.

I'm not inventing the wheel. Personality assessments, career guidance, and self-reflection have existed for generations. The challenge is assembling familiar concepts into a digital experience that feels genuinely useful.

Technology should create insight, not dependency

I find it strange that so much self-improvement software measures its success by how frequently someone returns.

If an application genuinely helps you improve your life, shouldn't you eventually need it less?

My ambition is to design around something harder to quantify than daily engagement: the occasional epiphany. That moment when a question, a connection, or an unexpected possibility fundamentally changes how you understand yourself.

There's something fascinating about combining centuries-old philosophical ideas with modern computational systems. Not because software possesses wisdom, but because thoughtful engineering and design can make meaningful information easier to explore.

SHROOMS is still evolving. Some ideas will work, others won't, and the product will undoubtedly change through testing.

But the objective remains simple: I don't want to build another application that occupies space on your phone while accomplishing absolutely nothing.

I want to build something that leaves you with more than you arrived with.

And if it helps even one person discover a possibility they hadn't previously considered, that's a better outcome than another meaningless streak.
